Machine-learning (ML) and explainable-AI (XAI) models are increasingly used to map arsenic, fluoride, and nitrate in groundwater, and many report high accuracy. That accuracy is usually measured on randomly selected test wells that sit close to training wells. We tested how much of it survives when a model must predict where it has no data, using six datasets with about 117,000 wells and water sources from the USA, Bangladesh, Pakistan, and Türkiye, including new field data from the Lower Indus Basin (848 wells, 2024) and the Kazan Basin (59 samples, 2021–2022). Replacing random cross-validation with spatial-block validation lowered R2 by 22–71% in four open datasets, and leave-one-district-out validation turned Lower Indus fluoride and nitrate models from weakly positive to worse than predicting the mean. The gap persisted with the geological, soil, and climate predictors of a published national arsenic model (ROC-AUC 0.87 to 0.66 for unseen ecoregions) and across five algorithms, from random forests to neural networks. Hydrochemistry measured in the same well inflated skill (R2 up to 0.81) but cannot map unsampled wells, a form of predictor leakage. SHAP rankings changed with the training districts, 90% prediction intervals covered only 72–84% of wells in new regions, and a Pakistan-trained arsenic model flagged 2% of Kazan Basin samples as unsafe when 84% were. We propose a five-stage framework and a reporting checklist for next-generation, explainable, and transferable groundwater AI.
